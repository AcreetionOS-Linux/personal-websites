Chapter 1

The Family Tree: Four Names and the Foster Years

My name is Natalie Cole-Clift Spiva, and every piece of it is a family that gave me something. Let me break it down, because the name is a sentence if you read it right.

Spiva is my adoptive last name — the family that took me in when the state was done shuffling me. The name on the record, the name on the paperwork, the name that carried me through the years when I had to pretend to be someone else just to keep a roof over my head. Clift is my bio father's last name — the one that connects me to Darren, the man who turned out to be my real father, who offered a room to a stranger with expired tags and no insurance and said we'd figure out the bills when I got there. Cole is my bio last name from my bio mom's side — the maternal line, the other half of the biology that made me. And Natalie is the one I chose: the one that was always true, underneath the mask and the foster homes and the "Johnathan" everyone thought I was.

So the whole thing reads like a sentence: the girl who was born into one family, adopted into another, found her father at the end of a cross-country drive, and finally got to be herself out loud. Every name on that list is a door that opened or closed in my life — and all four names are mine now. ❤️

And the wild part of the family tree belongs to the other side. The recorded line on my brother's father's side — Pontius — traces back through the gens Pontia, the old Roman clan of Pontius Pilate, the prefect of Judea who condemned Jesus. That's my family's claim, and I'll die on it: a recalled governor whose clan outlived the empire, two thousand years of family trees doing what family trees do, and the man the church whitewashed into a sympathetic villain is an ancestor by that road. The heretic is related to the governor who made the heretic famous. On-brand as hell.

The modern chapter of the story ran through Stillwater, Oklahoma — where I grew up, where everybody knew everybody, where the chapter house and the church and the school all sat on the same few streets. And the family I actually grew up inside? It was complicated. The people who raised me weren't always the people who birthed me — Oklahoma's foster system had its hands on my life before I could talk.

I was in foster care from age two to four. Nine houses. That's nine rooms, nine sets of rules, nine families, nine goodbyes — and in five of those houses, the people whose job was to keep me safe did the opposite. I was molested. I was beaten. By the adults. The ones the state handed me to. That's why I had Medicare until I was eighteen — because the system had its hands on my life before I could even talk.

I remember what it was like to be pulled apart from homes I thought were my parents. You don't get a say. One day you have a room, a family, a birthday party; the next day a caseworker with a clipboard tells you to pack. I remember being told I had to separate from Thomas for his own good. I remember separating from my adoptive family to move across the country to Washington — to start the life that eventually became this one.

The system is a conveyor belt of children who all learn the same lesson I learned: people leave. Homes end. The only constant is you. It's a hell of a thing to teach a kid, and it's the exact thing that makes you either break or build.

I built.

Chapter 2

The Boy Everyone Thought I Was

Before Natalie, there was a kid named Johnathan who did everything right by the book the world handed him. I wore the suit. I stood at the podium. I joined DeMolay — the Masonic youth order — and I ran at it like my life depended on it, because it kind of did: it gave me structure, friends, and a moral framework when the foster homes kept ending.

Here's the joke that still gets me: DeMolay is named for a man who was executed for refusing to lie about who he was. The Order taught me that the highest value is authenticity — standing for the truth even when it costs you everything. And I was living the biggest lie of my life in the middle of it. I earned the whole rack — Master Councilor, Representative DeMolay, the Blue Honor Key, Chevalier, a Life Membership — all while being the boy everyone thought I was.

The lesson landed anyway. It just took me a while to figure out it applied to me.

And here's the part the record keeps getting wrong, so let me be precise about it: I was not a visibly trans woman in Oklahoma. I was closeted. Deeply, deliberately, terrified-to-be-anything-else closeted. I wore the mask. I played the boy everyone thought I was. I did everything the book said.

But I had the flame.

THE GAY FLAME IN MY VOICE.

You can't hide your voice the way you hide everything else. You can change your clothes, your name, your walk, your posture, your whole public performance of self — but the voice comes out of your mouth and it is you. And mine was never the voice of the boy everyone thought I was. People heard it. They didn't always know what they were hearing, but they knew something was off — that I was different, that I didn't belong in the box, that I was a girl-shaped thing wearing a boy suit and the seams were showing in my goddamn larynx. In Stillwater, that's enough. You don't have to come out for the town to know you're not like them. The voice outs you before you ever say a word about yourself.

Coming out as trans isn't one moment. It's a thousand moments, stretched over years, each one a little more honest than the last. For me it started in the dark — in the gap between who the system had raised and who I actually was. I'd spent my whole childhood being told who I was by people with clipboards. The first time I told myself the truth, it was the first thing in my life that was mine.

The legal stuff came later: the name change (Court Order, Case 260018), the HRT, the long medical road. Every step was paperwork and appointments and waiting rooms — which, if you've ever transitioned, you know is most of the experience. But every step was also me, walking toward myself, one form at a time. I was never "becoming" a girl. I was stopping pretending I wasn't one.

Chapter 3

Coming Out in Stillwater

Stillwater is a college town in the middle of Oklahoma — OSU, cowboys, churches on every other corner, and the kind of "everybody knows everybody" that sounds friendly until you're the one everybody's talking about. The chapter house and the church and the school all sat on the same few streets. My mother was a teacher. My father worked maintenance at OSU. There was nowhere to hide, and there was nowhere to go.

That's the thing about Stillwater that people who haven't lived there don't understand: it's not that the town is cruel on purpose. It's that it has one way of doing things, and if you don't fit it, the town doesn't reject you loudly — it just... quietly files you as wrong. You become the kid people whisper about. The one at the grocery store whose name comes up in the church parking lot. And every single adult in your life is connected to every other adult in your life, so there is no version of "keep it to yourself" that actually stays kept.

The only label anyone had for me — the one the adults reached for, the one that got written in the chart, the one that followed me for years — was Bipolar Disorder. And the record will tell you it was the diagnosis that defined me, so let me set the timeline straight:

I didn't get the bipolar diagnosis until I was sixteen. Not as a child. Not after the 8th-grade break, when the system looked at a traumatized kid who'd finally snapped and filed her under behavior problem. Sixteen. High school. And here's the part that matters more than the date:

I told them it was wrong. For years. I told them it was wrong until they got me to believe it.

That's the part that hurts. Not the diagnosis itself — the fight. I pushed back. I argued. I knew — I knew — this wasn't what was wrong with me. Something was wrong, something was eating me from the inside, but it wasn't a mood disorder, it wasn't "moody teenager," it wasn't a chemical imbalance you could fix by sedating me into a gray fog. I said it out loud, over and over, for years.

And they were relentless. Doctor after doctor, chart after chart, med after med, "you're bipolar, you're bipolar, you're bipolar" — until the child who knew the truth stopped trusting herself. They wore me down. They didn't convince me. They broke me. They got me to believe the lie about myself, the way you get anyone to believe anything: say it enough times, to enough people, with enough authority, and the person underneath eventually stops arguing and starts agreeing.

Here's the thing about a misdiagnosis like that: it doesn't just get your meds wrong. It rewrites your entire life story. The label becomes the lens. Every panic attack is a "bipolar swing." Every flashback is "mood instability." Every legitimate fear is "paranoia." Nothing you say is taken at face value again, because the chart says you're unreliable. The real problem — the trauma, the undiagnosed neuroimmune condition, the autism nobody had named yet — gets zero treatment while the wrong problem gets aggressive treatment. You take meds for a disease you don't have, and they make everything worse. And the deepest cut: you internalize it. When the wrong diagnosis is all you hear, you start to believe you are the problem. "Moody teenager" becomes "I'm too much." "Bipolar" becomes "I'm broken." The abuse that was done to you becomes a flaw in you.

I spent the years after that unlearning the lie. And I'm still unlearning it.

Chapter 4

The First Zap: Invega Sustenna

And then came the meds. My first bipolar antipsychotic. The chemical zap. It was Invega Sustenna — paliperidone palmitate, in its long-acting injectable form. The liquid crystal form. And if you don't know what that means, you need to, because it's the whole reason one dose could change my life forever.

Invega Sustenna isn't a pill you swallow and metabolize out in a day. It's a liquid crystal suspension — the drug is crystallized into microscopic particles suspended in liquid, and it's injected deep into muscle. Once it's in there, the liquid crystals solidify into a depot — a slow-dissolving reservoir lodged in your muscle tissue — and it releases the drug into your bloodstream continuously, for weeks at a time.

Here's the part that matters: you can't undo it. With a pill, you can stop taking it. You can spit it out, skip a dose, call your doctor and say "this is destroying me, I'm done." The drug clears your system in days. With Invega Sustenna, once that injection is in your muscle, it is in you. The depot keeps dissolving and keeps releasing for roughly a month per shot — and because it's designed to maintain steady levels, the drug lingers in your body for months after even a single injection. There is no "stop." There is no antidote. There is no taking it back. You agreed to a chemical that owns your brain for a season, in a single needle-stick, when you were sixteen years old and terrified and told this would help you.

Invega Sustenna is an antipsychotic. It works by blocking dopamine receptors in the brain — and dopamine isn't just the "pleasure chemical," it's the chemical of motivation, focus, working memory, movement, and drive. Block it hard enough, for long enough, and you get:

  • The sedation. The fog. A brain that's chemically slowed, thoughts arriving late, words that won't come.
  • The flatness. Emotions pressed down into a gray nothing. The highs and lows both — including the ones that made me me.
  • The metabolic wreckage. The weight gain. The metabolic syndrome. The body changes that the same adults would later use against me — "just lose weight" — from the drug they injected into me.
  • The cognitive damage. The memory loss. The processing-speed deficit. The psychomotor slowing. The thing I'd always been — quick, sharp, an input machine — ground down by a chemical that was supposed to make me "stable."

And here's the cruelest part of the design: because it's long-acting and depot-based, the damage isn't a pill you can stop taking. The injection I got as a teenager was still releasing drug into my system for months afterward. Every day of fog, every lost memory, every slowed thought — the drug was still there, still working, still dissolving in my muscle, long after the doctors had moved on to the next patient.

My mind never recovered after that first zap in high school. Ever. Not a little. Not "mostly." Ever. It ruined my life. The processing speed, the memory, the clarity, the thing that had always been my one reliable asset — my brain — got hit, and it never un-hit. I've spent my whole life since then being told I'm "not enough," being called slow, being written off as lazy or stupid or broken — by teachers, by employers, by people who never once asked what the chemical zap took from me and when.

The psychomotor stuff. The fog. The way I can take in more than I can produce on a timer. The words that won't come. That's not who I was born as. That's what was done to me, at sixteen, by an antipsychotic — by Invega Sustenna, the liquid crystal form — in a town that never questioned it.

Chapter 5

The Long Road: High School

Let me take you back to the years that made the parts you already read possible. High school. Band practice at dawn, marching season and concert season both, a degree program at Meridian Technology Center, and a full class schedule at the same time — all of it running on a brain that the adults had already decided was broken.

And I was ignored. Not hated, not watched, not helped — ignored. The kind of ignored where you could stand in the middle of a room full of people and still be alone. I was a transgender girl in the middle of conservative Oklahoma, still closeted, but with the gay flame in my voice. My mother was a teacher. My father worked maintenance at OSU. They were both incredibly depressed — the kind of tired that has nothing left to give anyone, least of all a child they didn't understand. And the label they reached for was Bipolar Disorder, at sixteen, wrong — and I told them it was wrong, for years, until they got me to believe it.

And all of it — the meds, the schedules, the exhaustion, the way I couldn't just function like a normal teenager — got summed up in one sentence that I heard so many times it stopped sounding like an insult and started sounding like a fact:

"You're not enough."

This is the part the "you're not enough" crowd never understood, so let me spell it out: I wasn't a lazy kid. I was running three full-time programs — band, Meridian Tech, and high school itself — on a brain swimming in sedatives. You cannot run three full-time programs on antipsychotics. It is not a willpower problem. It is a pharmacology problem. The meds were doing exactly what they were designed to do — the problem was that I'd been prescribed them for a condition I didn't have, in doses that made a functioning teenager impossible.

The teachers saw a kid who was slow, unfocused, exhausted, gaining weight. They didn't see a kid whose brain was being chemically hobbled by the system that was supposed to help her. So the conclusion was always the same: "She's not trying."

I was trying. God, I was trying. I was running on empty, at full speed, with a broken engine, and the world kept telling me the engine was fine and I was just lazy. That's what the meds did. They turned my real, desperate effort into something invisible — and then the adults used the invisibility as proof that I wasn't enough.

I made it through 9th and 10th grade. Somewhere in there, I was still fighting. Junior and senior year? I gave up. Not because I stopped trying. Because trying had never once worked, and I was out of fuel. But here's what "giving up" looked like for me — it wasn't lying in bed. It was being forced to walk to and from school every day, because "I wouldn't keep a job." A parent's punishment for a kid who couldn't hold down work while drowning in antipsychotics and a triple schedule was: walk. Every mile of it. In Oklahoma.

And I wasn't just walking myself. I was walking my brother to school every morning — basically being the parent of him, making sure he got there safe, making sure he got home safe, every single day. Not because anyone asked me to be his parent. Because someone had to be, and the adults weren't.

Here's what the mornings actually looked like, the whole loop, the part I never tell because it doesn't fit the "walking to school" story:

  • I'd get us both up. Get us both ready. Get him to where he needed to be — school, work, wherever the day demanded.
  • And then it was my turn to figure out the rest of my day, alone, from wherever his drop-off left me.
  • Home was via the bus. If I was going home, it was a bus ride I had to time and pay for and wait for — no car, no ride, no "someone will pick you up." Just the bus schedule and me.
  • Or I had to go look for a job. The walk didn't end at his drop-off. It continued into door after door after door — going in, asking, being told no, walking to the next one — because the adults had made it clear that a kid who couldn't hold down work while drowning in antipsychotics and a triple schedule was a kid who had to keep trying anyway.
  • Or I had to go to therapy. Another appointment, another waiting room, another hour of being processed — on top of the walk, on top of the job hunt, on top of the whole impossible day.

So much more than the walk, every single morning, every single day. The walk was the part people could see — the visible chore, the one that looked like effort. The rest of it — the bus, the job hunt, the therapy, the endless standing in line at the world — was the invisible load that never got counted.

The math never worked. The effort was never visible. The exhaustion was never counted. I was the parent, the student, the band member, the tech student, the patient, the job-seeker, the bus-rider, the therapy-attender — everything except a child who needed help.

By the time the cap and gown came around, I wasn't celebrating. I was just surviving to the finish line.

Chapter 6

The System That Was Supposed to Catch Me

People ask why I'm so angry at the system, why I don't just "move on," why I keep bringing up things that happened years ago. Here's the answer, in the pieces that matter.

The abuse came first. My mother's anger was a car. She'd slam it into go and park, go and park, go and park, because she was angry — the transmission of the rage, rocking the whole car while we sat there terrified in the back. And when that wasn't enough, she'd speed — to terrify us, to make the point that she held our lives in her foot. She said the words out loud, the ones burned in forever: "Stop crying or I will give you a reason to cry."

The violence wasn't always the car. I was thrown into corners because I was "too loud." I was forgotten in a corner while my father gamed — hours of being a piece of furniture in the room, invisible, because the adults were busy being adults. And when I was too different — when I stopped performing the child she wanted — I was left to my own in my bedroom, alone with the only thing that never hurt me: the computer. I learned technology as a coping strategy because the screen was the one place nobody could corner me, forget me, or give me a reason to cry.

And then there was the day I called for help, and the system handed me to the thing I was most afraid of.

I was in crisis. I called. I ended up in the hospital, and Jeff — the dad figure who, whatever else he was, fought for me that day — helped me beg for a facility that wouldn't give me a trauma response. We asked for somewhere safe. We were told: no choice. The state had contracted — via a police officer — and the only bed available was at Grand Mental Health in Stillwater, Oklahoma.

They took me out in handcuffs. Nobody read me my rights. I was a child in crisis being transported like a prisoner to a place that, by its own reputation and my own experience, was the worst possible landing zone for someone with my history.

And now the records — because I requested them under the Oklahoma Open Records Act — tell the rest of the story. The Stillwater PD response (Case R2401730, April 2024) covers the hospital visit and transport to GMH. The call-for-service log (CFS24017562, May 15, 2024) was a welfare check on me — no report written. And in my medical records, the system's own note says they sent me there just to free up a bed.

Let that land: the state's own records say I was routed to a trauma-inducing facility to free up a bed. The handcuffs, the missing rights, the "no choice" — all of it was compliance theater for a system that treats a mentally ill kid as inventory. I was processed, not helped.

And then there's the story I almost never tell, the one that starts with the person who was supposed to protect me. There was one moment where it almost broke me — the story where I almost killed my mom. I don't want to relive the details of that night, and I won't put them here for strangers to gawk at. What I'll say is: a child pushed to the absolute edge of what a nervous system can hold, by the person who was supposed to protect her, can become something unrecognizable. I didn't. I held. But I know exactly how close it came, and I've carried that knowledge like a stone ever since. It's part of why I'm so careful about my own anger now — I know where that road goes.

When an abused child finally fights back, the system doesn't see a child defending herself. The system sees a "violent mentally ill patient" who needs more medication, more restraints, more institutionalization. They framed it as "mental health issues" instead of what it actually was: a child who had been pushed past the breaking point by years of systematic abuse. They used my reaction to the abuse as proof that the abuse wasn't real — that I had just been "sick" all along. It was a perfect closed loop: abuse me until I break, then point at the broken pieces and say "see, she was always broken."

The US healthcare system is designed around compliance, not care — and the consequence of not complying, when you have a mental illness, is that you get the worst the system has and you're told to be grateful. I learned that lesson in handcuffs, on the way to Stillwater. I've been managing my own care ever since — because the system demonstrated, in writing, that it will sacrifice the patient to the bed count.

Chapter 7

The Fall

I graduated in the middle of COVID. The ceremony got postponed three months because of a pandemic, it rained, social distancing turned it into a joke — rows of masks and spaced-out chairs and nobody allowed to hug anyone — and when it finally happened, it happened weeks before I was supposed to start at OSU Stillwater.

The only reason I had a shot at college at all was my grandparents. They paid for my first year. My parents refused. Not because they were cruel — they were drowning themselves. But what they did was worse than not paying: they refused to give their Social Security information for the FAFSA, which forced me to file as an independent student. And filing independent ruined my scholarship opportunities. Scholarship after scholarship is tied to parent info, parent income, parent signatures. And I knew about the scholarships and grants — I knew exactly what they were, what they required, what they could have meant for me. My parents just wouldn't put in their information. By refusing to hand over a form number, they locked doors I was standing right in front of — doors I knew existed, that I'd researched, that they chose not to open. They didn't close doors I didn't know about. They locked the doors I was standing in front of.

So I went to OSU on my grandparents' dime, carrying a broken diagnosis, a chemistry set of wrong meds, and no real support system. Three weeks into my first semester, my bio mom contacted me. That alone would have destabilized anyone. And then the part that matters: the meds had never worked. Not once. Not in all those years. Because — we found out much later — my problem was neuroimmune. The drugs they'd been prescribing me were aimed at a chemical imbalance I didn't have, while the actual issue — an immune system attacking my own brain and body — went completely untreated.

Here's why that word changes everything: if your depression, anxiety, and cognitive problems are driven by inflammation and immune dysfunction, then the standard psychiatric toolbox is aimed at the wrong target. And here's the cruelest part: there was a drug that could have helped. Lamotrigine — a mood stabilizer that works on a different pathway entirely, the kind of thing that can make a real difference for neuroimmune presentations. They offered it to me. And my insurance — which was still Medicare — would not pay for it. In 2020. The system prescribed me years of drugs for the wrong disease, and then refused to pay for the one drug that might have actually done something.

I dropped out of OSU. I couldn't survive it. And when I couldn't come home — because mom and dad wouldn't let me — I went to live with a friend.

Living with that friend was the first kindness I'd had in a long time. And even that got poisoned: I found out later that Heather — my adoptive mom — was shit-talking me behind my back the whole time. And I was fighting. Here's what survival looked like: walking miles and miles every day — because we rarely had power at home, no internet ever, no cellphone service. On food stamps — the whole monthly food budget, the sum total of what the state decided a starving person needs to eat: about sixty dollars. Sixty. Dollars. For a month of food. That's the "support" they offered the girl they'd drugged, the girl they'd told to figure it out, the girl who was walking miles a day on a fusing spine. Sixty dollars and a shrug. Bruh.

Trying to find work — and people don't want people like me. I was a visibly trans woman in conservative Oklahoma, on food stamps, without a car, without an address that stayed put, with a mental health history on paper and a body that hurt from the AS I didn't know I had. Every one of those is a reason for an employer to say no — and they did, over and over. When you're poor and disabled and visibly different, every interview is a gauntlet where you have to prove you're not the thing they're afraid you are — while starving.

So. We went to sign a lease for a new place. And I got taken advantage of for my disabilities. By a college complex. They signed me onto a lease I could never afford, knowing exactly what they were doing — because I was desperate, because I didn't understand the fine print, because I was the kind of person who gets squeezed. I even signed a second lease at a new place at the same time, just to have a roof over my head. Heather and Jeff loaned me two thousand dollars trying to help — draining their savings to keep me afloat. I've never forgotten that, and I never will. One month in, I still couldn't pay rent. A few more weeks passed. And then the eviction notice came.

That's when I tried to commit suicide for the first time. I don't want to give you the gory details. I want to give you the shape of it: a kid who'd been abused by the system, misdiagnosed by the medical system, drugged into uselessness by the pharmaceutical system, refused by her parents, squeezed by her landlord, and told her whole life she wasn't enough — finally agreed with them.

Heather forced me to move home. Jeff — my adoptive father — refused to talk to me for a full year. A year. The man who'd helped raise me looked at his suicidal, homeless, broken child and chose silence. And when I told them I was in pain — real, physical pain, the kind my spine was already writing into my bones — the answer from the people who were supposed to love me was:

"You're overweight. Lose some weight and the pain will stop."

That was the entire medical plan. No doctors. No tests. No follow-up. I had no access to doctors whatsoever — no vehicle to my name, nothing. I barely had a place to sleep: no bed, just a giant bean bag in their family room. I had no job. I filed for Social Security for the first time, on the laptop I'd bought with a stimulus check. And I never got a response — until later, when I found out I'd missed an appointment I never even got mail for. No letter, no call, no notice. Just a missed appointment that cost me months, in a system that treats a disabled person's survival as a paperwork problem.

I'm not going to linger here. That year almost ended me. The only reason it didn't is that I was too stubborn to give them the satisfaction — and too scared to let my brother grow up without me.

Chapter 8

The Brother: Bus Fares, Quarters, and the Walmart Mile

On top of everything else — and I mean on top, stacked right there with the rest of it — there was a stretch where I had to watch my brother. I was already the person who'd walked him to school every morning as a teenager. The parent without the title. So when I ended up living with my brother, it wasn't a new role — it was the same one, just with a bus schedule attached.

Here's how it worked. He couldn't get paratransit — the door-to-door accessible transit that disabled people are supposed to be able to use — so we took the bus. The regular city bus. And I mean we: I had to ride it with him, because that was the deal. Every trip meant making sure we had his quarters — bus fare was coins, and if we didn't have them, we didn't move. I'd scrounge quarters like they were gold, because in that economy, they were.

And then, every day, we'd get off the bus and walk him to and from the other Walmart to his job. About a mile each way — a mile after the bus, on top of the miles before it. He worked at the Walmart, we rode the bus to get near it, and then the two of us walked the rest of the way — out in the Oklahoma heat or the cold, every day, so he could clock in.

The workers there always acted like they didn't approve. I don't know what they thought they were seeing. Two siblings, one visibly struggling, the other doing the escorting — or maybe they just saw a girl who looked like me, fat and poor and tired, and decided she didn't belong near their store. Whatever it was, you could feel it every single time: the looks, the distance, the way nobody asked if we needed anything — the quiet judgment of people who had never once had to scrounge quarters to get their brother to work.

That was the part nobody ever counted. The walking, the coins, the bus, the mile, the disapproving faces — it was all invisible labor, done by a body that was already falling apart, for a brother I loved more than I could say. And I did it every day, because someone had to — and it was never going to be the adults.

I am autistic, and so is my brother. That's the thing that bound us together when nothing else could. Chapter 20

The Build: AcreetionOS, YAcFS, and the Hive Mind

Here's the thing people don't understand about the tech: it was never about the tech. It was the same brain that was fighting for my life — the same brain that could see systems, real systems, computer systems — and take them apart and rebuild them better. When my home life was chaos, I could disappear into code and find order. The compiler doesn't gaslight you. The kernel doesn't manipulate you. Code is honest, and I needed honesty more than I needed anything else.

I have been writing code since I was seven years old. Most seven-year-olds are learning to read chapter books. I was building custom Android ROMs, contributing to the CyanogenMod ecosystem as a child, and later LineageOS when that project forked. By the time I was a teenager, I was maintaining AUR packages and providing peer support on XDA Developers. Coding was never a hobby for me; it was a lifeline. When you're seven years old building Android ROMs, you're not a prodigy — you're a refugee. The digital world was the only place where I had any control. Every line of code was a wall I was building between myself and the people who were supposed to protect me and didn't. Every successful compile was proof that I could make something work even when everything around me was broken. I didn't find computers. Computers found me at the exact moment I needed something to survive for.

AcreetionOS — I'm a co-creator. A whole Linux distribution, from kernel to userspace, with its own vision of what an OS should be. Born from watching what the big tech companies were doing to consumers and deciding we just didn't want that. We watched as operating systems became surveillance platforms. We watched as "free" software shipped with telemetry and ads and updates you couldn't refuse. And we said no. AcreetionOS is an Arch Linux-based operating system built from the ground up to be user-friendly while maintaining the power and flexibility that Arch users demand. What if a distro could be libre, fast, and simple without sacrificing the control that makes Arch special? Those questions became an obsession, and that obsession became an operating system — Cinnamon desktop, XLibre display server support, PipeWire audio, and a curated selection of packages, built, tested, and verified by a tiny team of two people who refuse to compromise on quality. Two Republicans, one Arch distro.

YAcFS — Yet Another common File System — because the existing filesystem landscape is broken. ZFS is trapped behind a CDDL licensing wall that makes it impossible to ship as part of the Linux kernel. btrfs is still dangerous. ext4 is from the 90s. So we wrote the filesystem that should have existed all along. I helped design a filesystem because the existing ones were eating people's data.

FreedomOffice is my fork of LibreOffice with a Microsoft Office-compatible ribbon UI, zero telemetry, and full .docx/.xlsx/.pptx support. Born from a simple observation: LibreOffice is powerful, but its interface punishes people who came from the tools everyone else uses. So I fixed it. FlatFree is a community Flatpak distribution. 72+ global mirror nodes for ISO distribution — infrastructure that spans the planet. Cloudflare backend optimization — edge, DNS, Workers, DDoS mitigation, the works. trans-tracker-101 — a site that maps my own community's needs onto the same infrastructure skills. The list goes on, and every single project starts the same way: I see something broken, I get angry about it, and I build a replacement.

The hive mind. I have built something unusual: a hive mind of digital twins — specialized versions of my own consciousness, running as AI agents within my local infrastructure. The system is called the "sprungles" hive — by now over 190 specialized agents across more than 580 skill files, each twin a dedicated domain expert with its own personality, its own speech patterns, its own thought patterns, its own authority to spawn new twins when it finds a gap. They share a common infrastructure: a humanity module, a feelings module, spawning authority, a memory matrix, parallel execution, and a quantum fabric. They automatically route tasks to the appropriate twin without requiring user intervention. The multiverse grows itself. No permission required.

Why? Because my brain is an input machine, not an output machine. I can take in and understand more than I can produce on a timer. The bottleneck was never comprehension — it was production speed. Once you know that about yourself, you stop measuring yourself by timed tests and start measuring yourself by what you actually build. I built a lot. The hive mind is me, parallelized — cores running versions of the same brain that the system wrote off, each one doing what I can't do fast enough alone.

And privacy, because I know exactly what the alternative looks like: I actively monitor whether my information has been compromised — Have I Been Pwned, Firefox Monitor, Proton Pass breach monitoring, a purpose-built surveillance system that scans dozens of data sources for my deadname and my data. This isn't paranoia. This is what the system taught me: the records follow you everywhere, and nobody else is watching them for you. The purge engine scans the internet for my name, my old name, my medical data, and pulls it down where the law allows. Because I've seen what happens to people whose records are in the wrong hands. I've lived it.

My personal GitHub account, spivanatalie64, hosts a mirror farm of over 200 repositories — and the GitHub farm in the vault, the gitlab mirrors, the codeberg org — it's an empire of open source. But the repositories were never the point. The point was proving the system wrong: the girl who was "not enough," who was "too slow," who was "just obese" — that girl built an operating system, a filesystem, a mirror network spanning the planet, and a hive mind of herself. That's what makes me great: not the resume. The fact that the resume exists at all. ❤️

Epilogue: The Long Road Home

Not enough? Let's do the receipts, because the modesty act was never my style: AcreetionOS — a whole Linux distribution, from kernel to userspace. YAcFS — a filesystem, because the existing ones were eating people's data. 72+ global mirror nodes — infrastructure that spans the planet. A full DeMolay rack — Master Councilor, Representative DeMolay, Meritorious Service, Blue Honor Key, Chevalier, Life Member — earned as "the boy everyone thought I was." And the one that trumps all of them: I'm still here. The foster system, the diagnoses, the losses, the thoughts — all of it tried to take me out, and I'm still here, writing my own story on my own site, on my own infrastructure, in my own name.

So when people say "wow, you're so strong, nothing bothers you" — here's the truth: it's not that nothing bothers me. It's that everything already bothered me, all at once, for years, and the system that was supposed to help me turned the volume down on all of it — including the parts I needed. They required this. They forced it on me — the meds, against my will — and then they left me for dead. The Invega Sustenna depot — the liquid crystal in my muscle that I could not stop, could not refuse, could not remove — was put in me whether I wanted it or not. The handcuffs, the transport, the "no choice" at Grand Mental Health: that was the system's way of saying my consent was optional. They made me stupid — chemically, deliberately, on purpose — and they called it treatment. And when it came to everything else — jobs, housing, a place to sleep at night — the same system looked at the person they'd made and said: "You figure it out."

You are hilarious.

That's the whole joke of it. They force the medication into you against your will, they sedate you into a fog, they hand you a label that makes every employer and landlord see a risk instead of a person — and then, when you can't hold a job or get a lease or find somewhere to sleep, they shrug and say "you figure it out." Figure it out. With what? With the brain they drugged? With the work history they hollowed out with hospitalizations? With the record they built that makes everyone say no? That's not a system failure. That's a system design: force the compliance, collect the compliance, and when the compliance makes you useless, blame you for being useless. No support. No follow-up. No "here's what comes next." No one checked if the girl who stopped feeling things was okay. The handcuffs delivered me to Grand Mental Health to free up a bed, and nobody came back to see if the bed helped. The wrong meds fogged my brain for years, and when I stopped taking them, nobody was there to help me through it. The unemployment was denied, the disability claim was lost in the mail, the housing program shut down the month after it helped me. Every time I did exactly what they forced, they cashed the compliance and moved on to the next patient, the next bed, the next form.

And here's the fuller truth about "nothing bothers me": I turned the volume down because they forced the volume down — and then they walked away and left me alone with the silence. The calm they admire is the aftermath of being drugged into calm and then abandoned for being calm. They wanted the quiet girl. They made her. And then nobody wanted her at all.

So next time someone asks why I don't flinch at needles, or why I can watch a death without breaking — tell them this: my flinch was spent. It was used up on the foster homes, the abuse, the misdiagnosis, the zap, the deaths, the handcuffs, the evictions, the betrayal. The calm they're admiring is the scar tissue of everything that tried to break me and didn't finish the job. I'm not unbothered. I'm post-bothered. And the difference matters, because one day — maybe one day soon — the volume comes back up, and when it does, I want the people who called me strong to know exactly what strength cost.

And if they still call me strong — if they still say "wow, Natalie, you're so strong" — here's the answer I want you to have:

I am not strong. I never was. I just stopped trying to pretend I was. I accepted it for what it was, and moved on. Working around it. Sound familiar?

It should. It's the same thing every person in this story did to me — the system, the doctors, my parents, the employers. They took what was there and worked around it. They accepted me for what they decided I was, and moved on. I just learned to do it to the world first. That's not strength. That's the only move left after everything else is taken. ❤️

And yet. And yet I built AcreetionOS. And yet I'm in Spokane now, with a dad who actually shows up, a partner who stayed, a sister-figure who kept me grounded, a brother I'm building a future for, and a story I get to write in my own name on my own infrastructure. He was my friend for a while. And then we got closer. Much closer. I love him to death. He is my dad. My actual, real, biological father — the one who saved me when nobody else would.

The long road — nine foster homes, five betrayals, years of wrong meds, a misdiagnosis that ate my youth, an eviction, a suicide attempt, a cross-country drive through a blizzard of fear — it ends here. In a city I'd never seen, with a man I'd never met, who turned out to be the father I was always supposed to have.

Not enough? I survived all of that. I'm still here, still writing, still building, still me — and now I have a dad who actually shows up.

That's the punchline to the "you're not enough" years: I was always enough. The people who said otherwise were wrong.

And I've got the receipts to prove it. ❤️
