# Coming Out in Stillwater — The Real Story

*What actually happens when a girl with the flame comes out in Stillwater, Oklahoma. The real story. Every angle. No filter.*

---

## The town

Stillwater is a college town in the middle of Oklahoma — OSU, cowboys, churches on every other corner, and the kind of "everybody knows everybody" that sounds friendly until you're the one everybody's talking about. The chapter house and the church and the school all sat on the same few streets. My mother was a teacher. My father worked maintenance at OSU. There was nowhere to hide, and there was nowhere to go.

That's the thing about Stillwater that people who haven't lived there don't understand: it's not that the town is cruel on purpose. It's that it has **one way of doing things**, and if you don't fit it, the town doesn't reject you loudly — it just... quietly files you as wrong. You become the kid people whisper about. The one at the grocery store whose name comes up in the church parking lot. And every single adult in your life is connected to every other adult in your life, so there is no version of "keep it to yourself" that actually stays kept.

---

## The closet and the flame

Let me be precise about this, because the record keeps getting it wrong: **I was not a visibly trans woman in Oklahoma.** I was closeted. Deeply, deliberately, terrified-to-be-anything-else closeted. I wore the mask. I played the boy everyone thought I was. I did everything the book said.

But I had the flame.

**THE GAY FLAME IN MY VOICE.**

You can't hide your voice the way you hide everything else. You can change your clothes, your name, your walk, your posture, your whole public performance of self — but the voice comes out of your mouth and it *is* you. And mine was never the voice of the boy everyone thought I was. People heard it. They didn't always know what they were hearing, but they knew something was *off* — that I was different, that I didn't belong in the box, that I was a girl-shaped thing wearing a boy suit and the seams were showing in my goddamn larynx.

In Stillwater, that's enough. You don't have to come out for the town to know you're not like them. The voice outs you before you ever say a word about yourself. It's the tell you can't fix, can't mask, can't perform away. And the town *hears* it — in the classroom, in the band hall, in the job interview, in the church. They know. Maybe they can't name it. But they know.

---

## The diagnosis at sixteen

The only label anyone had for me — the one the adults reached for, the one that got written in the chart, the one that followed me for decades — was **Bipolar Disorder**. And the record will tell you it was the diagnosis that defined me, so let me set the timeline straight:

**I didn't get the bipolar diagnosis until I was sixteen.**

Not as a child. Not after the 8th-grade break, when the system looked at a traumatized kid who'd finally snapped and filed her under *behavior problem*. Sixteen. High school. And here's the part that matters more than the date:

**I told them it was wrong. For years. I told them it was wrong until they got me to believe it.**

That's the part that hurts. Not the diagnosis itself — the *fight*. I pushed back. I argued. I knew — I *knew* — this wasn't what was wrong with me. Something was wrong, something was eating me from the inside, but it wasn't a mood disorder, it wasn't "moody teenager," it wasn't a chemical imbalance you could fix by sedating me into a gray fog. I said it out loud, over and over, for years.

And they were relentless. Doctor after doctor, chart after chart, med after med, "you're bipolar, you're bipolar, you're bipolar" — until the child who knew the truth stopped trusting herself. They wore me down. They didn't convince me. They *broke* me. They got me to believe the lie about myself, the way you get anyone to believe anything: say it enough times, to enough people, with enough authority, and the person underneath eventually stops arguing and starts *agreeing*.

**That hurts.** Knowing you were right, and that being right wasn't enough. Knowing the truth was in you, and they crushed it anyway. I have a diagnosis I never should have carried, that I fought for years, that I only accepted because I ran out of strength to keep fighting. And then I carried it like it was mine.

## The first zap

And then came the meds.

My first bipolar antipsychotic. The chemical zap.

I don't know what was in that first one — I was sixteen and I trusted the people who were supposed to help me, and they handed me something that was supposed to make the "bipolar" go away. Instead it felt like my brain was being electrocuted from the inside. A chemical zap, right through my mind. And something in there never came back.

**My mind never recovered after that first zap in high school. Ever.**

Not a little. Not "mostly." **Ever.** It ruined my life. The processing speed, the memory, the clarity, the thing that had always been my one reliable asset — my brain — got hit, and it never un-hit. I've spent my whole life since then being told I'm "not enough," being called slow, being written off as lazy or stupid or broken — by teachers, by employers, by people who never once asked what the chemical zap took from me and when.

The psychomotor stuff. The fog. The way I can take in more than I can produce on a timer. The words that won't come. That's not who I was born as. That's what was *done* to me, at sixteen, by the first antipsychotic, in a town that never questioned it.

---

## The two breaks

People ask why I'm so angry at the system, why I don't just "move on," why I keep bringing up things that happened years ago. Here's the answer, in two pieces. **They broke me. Twice. My parents did.**

**Break one: the diagnosis.** I told them the bipolar diagnosis was wrong. For years. And instead of listening — instead of looking at the trauma, the abuse, the undiagnosed neuroimmune condition, the autism nobody had named yet — they pushed back harder. Until they got me to believe it myself. They didn't protect me from the wrong label. They *installed* it. That's not a mistake. That's a decision made over years, every single time they chose the label over the child.

**Break two: the scholarships.** The page used to say I found out about the scholarship doors "too late." That's wrong, and I'm correcting the record: **I knew about the scholarships and grants.** I knew exactly what they were, what they required, what they could have done for me. My parents just **wouldn't put in their information.** They refused to give their Social Security numbers for the FAFSA — which forced me to file as independent — which killed my scholarship eligibility, one door at a time.

I *knew.* That's what makes it unforgivable. This wasn't ignorance. This wasn't "we couldn't help." This was adults who knew exactly what a form number meant, who knew what their refusal would cost, and who made the choice anyway. They didn't close doors I didn't know about. **They locked the doors I was standing in front of.**

Twice. On purpose. And then they wondered why I was broken.

---

## The irony: lamotrigine

Here's the part that would be funny if it didn't make me want to scream:

**I mostly stabilized on lamotrigine.**

The mood stabilizer. The one drug that actually worked — the one that worked on the pathway my brain actually needed, the neuroimmune-adjacent thing I'd been trying to tell them about for years. They **offered it to me.** And my insurance — Medicare, 2020 — **would not pay for it.**

They prescribed me years of antipsychotics for a disease I was telling them I didn't have. The first one chemically zapped my mind and it never recovered. And then, when they finally — *finally* — got to the drug that could actually have helped, the system said no. Because it was expensive. Because the algorithm said so. Because compliance and cost matter more than care.

**THE FUCKING IRONY.** The drug that saved me is the one they refused to pay for. The treatment that worked is the one I had to fight to get, or get without them, or get too late. And by the time I stabilized on it — by the time the mood swings finally settled — the damage from the wrong meds was already done. The zap had already fired. The mind had already been hit.

I'm mostly stable now. "Mostly." And I have pretty much dementia at this point — the memory, the fog, the cognition — from years of wrong treatment, from the chemical zap, from the antipsychotics that were prescribed for a diagnosis I told them was wrong.

**I am not allowed to live alone or do my own meds for these reasons.**

Let that land. The system that refused to listen to me, that misdiagnosed me, that zapped me, that denied the drug that could have helped — that same system now looks at the results of its own work and says I'm too impaired to live alone or manage my own medications. They broke the brain and then used the breakage as proof they were right to break it.

## The consequences, from every angle

Let me lay it out, because "what happens to a girl who comes out — or gets read — in Stillwater, Oklahoma" deserves the full inventory:

**Family.** Your parents already have you filed as "moody," as "too much," as "difficult." The flame makes it worse — you're visibly, audibly wrong in ways they can't fix or explain. And the people who are supposed to protect you are the ones who hand you the label, refuse the form number, and tell you to lose weight when you say your spine is fusing.

**School.** The teachers see a kid who's slow, unfocused, exhausted, gaining weight from the meds. They don't ask what the meds are doing. They conclude you're not trying. In a town where everybody knows everybody, that conclusion travels.

**The medical system.** You tell them the diagnosis is wrong. They don't listen. You tell them your back hurts. They say "you're just obese." You get taken to **one spine appointment** and never again. The AS fuses your spine while the system argues with you about your weight.

**The mental health system.** Stillwater's own. When I was in crisis, I was taken to **Grand Mental Health in Stillwater** — in handcuffs, nobody read me my rights, and the state's own records (Stillwater PD Case **R2401730**, April 2024; call-for-service log **CFS24017562**, May 15, 2024) show I was routed there *just to free up a bed*. A child in crisis, processed like inventory, by the system that was supposed to catch her.

**Legal and records.** The wrong diagnosis follows you onto paper, and paper follows you everywhere — into job interviews, into housing applications, into the mouths of people who get to decide if you're "stable enough" to exist in public.

**Employment.** The gaps from the hospitalizations. The body that hurts. The voice that reads as wrong. The meds that slow your brain. Every interview is a gauntlet where you have to prove you're not the thing they're afraid you are — while starving. Ross, Belk, the janitor job, and the hotel: a **Holiday Inn Express run by the subcontractor Shree Sani Sai**, which paid me $9.50 an hour for 110-hour weeks and gave me $800 a month — roughly $3,700 a month short, **north of $20,000 stolen over the year**, with no car so I walked three miles on a fusing spine to deposit each stolen check and two miles to Aldi for groceries, begging rides from the very people shorting me. When I left because a boss's child sexually advanced on me and I said no, Oklahoma denied my unemployment — Title 40 §2-404, "voluntary quit without good cause," the good cause being unprovable because there was no one safe to report the boss's child to. I was cheated because I couldn't afford to question it, and I couldn't afford to question it because they'd already made sure I couldn't work anywhere else.

**Housing.** Evictions. A rat-infested studio I put four thousand dollars cash down on just to have a roof. A college complex that signed me onto a lease I could never afford, because desperate people get squeezed. Walking two miles each way to work with a fusing spine because "not having a car" is a character flaw, apparently.

**Church and community.** The town hears the flame. The town files you as wrong. The town's adults are all connected, and the whispers do the work that outright cruelty doesn't have to. You're not chased out of Stillwater. You're *squeezed* out — by a thousand small refusals, a thousand doors locked by people who'll never admit they locked them.

**The long tail.** A suicide attempt. A cross-country drive with expired tags and no insurance. Grand Lake Mental Health's housing program — which shut down right after it helped me. A pedophilia accusation that never happened but didn't need to be true — made by the Stillwater Chapter's **assistant chapter dad**, the adult in authority over the youth order I'd given everything to, who is now the **main Chapter Dad**; the minute lawyers got involved it all stopped, charges were dropped, and they even invited me back afterward (from my memory — I don't rely entirely on it for details, but that's the shape of it). And a present where I'm not allowed to live alone or do my own meds, because the system that did all of this now gets to say I'm not competent.

## The line

So when people ask what happens to a trans girl who comes out in Stillwater, Oklahoma — or who doesn't come out, but gets read anyway, because the flame is in her voice and there's nowhere to hide — this is what happens. She gets mislabeled. She gets medicated into fog. She gets denied the drug that would help. She gets told she's not enough until she agrees. She gets locked out of the scholarships she knew about. She gets handcuffed on the way to the bed they freed up for her. She gets squeezed out of town by a thousand small refusals.

And if she's lucky — if she's stubborn, if someone loans her five hundred dollars, if a stranger on the internet turns out to be her real father — she gets out. She builds an operating system. She writes the truth down where the record can't bury it.

I'm mostly stable now. I'm still here. And the diagnosis I fought for years? **I was right to fight it.** The only reason I carry it at all is that they broke me before I could stop them.

That's the real story. Every angle. ❤️
